Sentinel

A corpus-scale audit engine for biomedical research publications. It checks whether a paper's references exist, whether they have been retracted, whether its own statistics are internally consistent, whether it reported the outcome it registered, and whether it says what its sources actually say.

1 in 277Papers with a fabricated reference, early 2026
~31%Trials whose primary outcome changed between registration and publication
1 in 8Papers with a p-value error large enough to flip the conclusion

Sources: Lancet audit of 2 million papers and 97 million citations (2026); systematic reviews of registry-to-publication outcome consistency; statcheck analysis of 250,000 reported p-values. Three different defects, three different checks, all detectable from public data.

The problem

Why this exists

Clinical decisions rest on a chain. A trial is run, results are published, the publication is pooled into a systematic review, the review becomes a guideline, the guideline changes practice. Errors enter at every link and almost never get caught.

A trial changes its primary outcome after seeing the data. A paper reports a p-value that contradicts its own test statistic. A review cites a study retracted two years earlier. A guideline cites a paper for a claim that paper never made.

Every one of those failures is publicly auditable. The registry, the publication, the retraction database, and the payment records are all open. Nobody audits them because checking requires reading two documents side by side, it takes an expert hours per paper, and there are millions of papers.

The evidence base underlying clinical practice contains detectable, quantifiable defects that nobody checks for, because checking requires reading two documents side by side and nobody has time. That is an automation problem, not a medical one.

Primary user: systematic review teams. A review takes one to two years, and screening plus quality appraisal is the bottleneck. Guideline committees and journal editors are the adjacent buyers. The output is designed for human triage, never for automatic consumption.

Scope

What the system decides, and what it never decides

Sentinel makes one kind of judgment: whether two documents that should agree actually agree. Registered outcome against published outcome. Citing sentence against cited source. Reported p-value against reported test statistic. It never judges whether a clinical claim is true, never recommends treatment, and never replaces expert appraisal.

This is why the project needs no medical training to build. Comparing two texts for consistency is a language and systems problem. It is also why it touches no patient data: every source is public bibliographic metadata, registry records, and published full text. No protected health information, no regulatory scope, no clinical validation burden.

The two that carry the project

Outcome switching is the core. Both sides of the comparison are public and structured, the matching is genuinely semantic rather than string-based — deciding whether "change in HbA1c at 12 weeks" and "glycemic control at 3 months" are the same outcome is a language problem — and the documented prevalence gives an external validation target. It has a clinical consequence statable in one line: trials that switch outcomes report effect sizes roughly 16% larger.

Claim support is the hard tier and shares all its machinery with outcome switching. Same entailment models, same retrieval, same evaluation harness.

Corpus audit, not single-paper lookup

Sentinel runs over thousands of papers and reports prevalence. A single-paper endpoint exists for demonstration, but the system is built for the corpus. Throughput, batching, concurrency, cost per unit, caching, queueing, partial failure, and retry semantics only become real problems at 400,000 verifications rather than 40 — and those are exactly the problems the project exists to demonstrate.

Thirteen tasks are load-bearing in the core build. The multimodal group is not decoration: effect sizes routinely live in Table 2 or a forest plot and nowhere in the prose, so a verifier that reads only text cannot check the claims that matter most.

Deliberately unused: every generative vision and audio task, depth estimation, keypoint detection, video classification, reinforcement learning, and robotics. Listing what a system does not use is part of showing it was designed rather than assembled.

The four modelling additions

Each is required by the product, not added for a resume

Calibration

The model outputs a confidence and the system thresholds it to control how many items reach a human. If that confidence is not calibrated, the threshold is arbitrary. Build reliability diagrams, apply Platt scaling and isotonic regression, and report expected calibration error before and after.

Rarely present in portfolios, and here the product breaks without it.

Distillation

Use the expensive LLM judge to label a large set, then train a small encoder on those labels. Measure both gaps: accuracy retained and cost reduced.

The single highest-value addition. One piece of work that reads as a training exercise to a machine learning interviewer and as a fifty-to-hundred-fold cost reduction to an AI engineering interviewer.

Class imbalance

Fabricated references run near 1%. At that base rate a model predicting "clean" every time scores 99% accuracy. Precision-recall curves rather than ROC, threshold optimisation against an explicit cost function, cost-sensitive loss, and a comparison of resampling strategies.

This is the modelling conversation an interviewer wants to have, and it is forced by the data rather than chosen.

The MLOps loop

Model registry with versioned artifacts and lineage, input-distribution drift detection, an automated retraining trigger, and rollback. MLflow for tracking, with the registry and drift monitors built on top.

Closes the gap between "I trained a model" and "I operate models."

Evaluation

The differentiator, and the reason to build this rather than something else

Ground truth comes from three independent directions, which is unusual for a portfolio project.

  • Programmatic corruption. Take verified true pairs and corrupt them systematically — swap the source, negate the direction, weaken or strengthen the effect, alter the population. Thousands of labelled examples at controlled difficulty, each with a known corruption type for error analysis.
  • Published annotated corpora. Human-labelled citation integrity data as an external check.
  • Hand-labelled holdout. Two hundred cases labelled manually, selected by active learning rather than at random.

Verdict accuracyMacro F1 across three classesInsufficient-evidence is a real class, not a failure to answer.
Synthetic-to-real gapBoth numbers, always togetherPublished work saw a pipeline fall from 97% on generated benchmarks to 90% on real cases, with one frontier model collapsing from 96% to 33% F1. Reporting only the flattering number is the tell of an unserious system.

CoverageShare of citations reaching a full-confidence verdictA verifier working on 30% of citations is a different product from one working on 90%. Coverage sits beside accuracy, never behind it.
RetrievalnDCG@10, Recall@50 with a four-row ablationBM25, dense, fusion, fusion plus rerank. One table, one chart.

At a 1% flag rate over 420,000 citations you hand humans 4,200 items. If a third are false positives, the tool is abandoned within a month. This inverts the usual priority: tune for precision, accept missed cases, and say so out loud.

Scale

Cascade, do not uniform-process

Running the expensive tier on every citation is what makes this look unaffordable. The architecture is a funnel where each stage costs roughly ten to thirty times the last, and only suspicion escalates.

StageVolume on 10k papersUnit costEscalation trigger
Deterministic checks~420,000Local lookup, sub-millisecondUnresolvable, retracted, statistically inconsistent
Cross-document~40,000Embedding plus classifierTrial-linked papers, suspected duplicates
Claim grounding, text~15,000Retrieval plus entailmentFlagged above, random audit sample, high-stakes claims
Claim grounding, visual~1,500Vision model over pagesNumeric claim absent from prose

Expensive work drops from roughly 420,000 units to 15,000, about a twenty-eight-fold reduction. The reference universe is large — OpenAlex holds around 250 million works — but it is a lookup table you query, not a corpus you process. The bounded object is the citation being verified.

The validation run

Ten thousand papers from the PubMed Central open-access subset, every reference and every statistic checked. Free data, no permissions, publishable result. Then compare measured prevalence against the published estimates. Landing near them validates the system against ground truth nobody constructed for it; landing far off is either a bug or a finding, and both are worth writing up.

What breaks first

Not compute. In order: full-text access, since claim grounding needs the cited source and much of biomedicine is paywalled — scope it to open access and degrade elsewhere to an explicitly labelled abstract-only verdict. Then precision. Then entity resolution, which degrades worse than linearly and will consume more time than the models do.

Build plan

Fifteen weeks, solo, shippable at every phase

Weeks 1–3

Bulk snapshot ingestion of Crossref, OpenAlex, Retraction Watch, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Europe PMC into Postgres. Reference parsing and staged entity resolution. All five deterministic checks. Fixture corpus with hand-verified expected findings.

Ships: real findings on real papers, zero false positives on the clean fixtures, no machine learning.

Weeks 4–6

Full-text acquisition for the open-access subset. pgvector index, hybrid retrieval with reciprocal rank fusion, cross-encoder reranking. Claim skeleton extraction. The corruption generator. Retrieval eval harness. The tabular baseline with feature engineering and SHAP.

Ships: the four-row retrieval ablation and a labelled synthetic evaluation set.

Weeks 7–9

Outcome switching: outcome-equivalence matching between registry versions and publication. Entailment models — zero-shot, fine-tuned, LLM judge, ensemble. Class imbalance handling and threshold optimisation. Calibration with reliability diagrams.

Ships: measured outcome-switching prevalence, calibrated confidences, the synthetic-to-real gap reported.

Weeks 10–12

Distillation of the judge into a small encoder, with the accuracy-versus-cost curve. LangGraph orchestration and the reviewer interrupt. Langfuse tracing, Ragas and promptfoo in CI. Model registry, drift detection, retraining trigger, rollback.

Ships: the distilled model with both gaps quantified, and eval-gated CI.

Weeks 13–15

Visual evidence: page retrieval and vision model reading of tables and figures. Cascade tuning and cost instrumentation. The ten-thousand-paper validation run. Adversarial suite, Docker Compose, MCP server, and the write-ups.

Ships: the deployed system, the validated corpus result, the evaluation report, and the post-mortem.

Cut list in order if the schedule slips: graph propagation, translation, visual question answering, local model serving. Protect the evaluation harness and the distillation work — they carry both career tracks.

Career mapping

One project, two emphases

The two markets share roughly two thirds of their top skills, so the project serves both. The work does not change; what leads does.

TargetLead withExample claim

Both statements describe the same afternoon's work.

The five deliverables that matter

  1. A repository with a real README — architecture diagram, ablation tables, reproducible setup.
  2. An evaluation report with every metric, synthetic and real, plus error analysis by failure type.
  3. A deployed instance someone can use without cloning anything.
  4. A write-up of the validation run against published prevalence estimates.
  5. A post-mortem of what did not work — the model that underperformed, the retrieval strategy abandoned, the cost blowup fixed.

Most candidates ship the first and stop. The second and fifth are what convince a hiring manager you have shipped something before.

What this project does not prove

Distributed training, GPU and kernel optimisation, high-volume real-time serving, and predictive modelling on proprietary business data — fraud, churn, lifetime value, demand forecasting. If roles of that kind are in the target set, they need a separate three-week project on a public dataset, done end to end. One deep project plus one focused one beats two shallow ones.

Impact metrics

Templates to fill with measured numbers — every one is produced by the harness

  • Built a corpus-scale evidence integrity engine auditing [N] references and [M] reported statistics across [P] biomedical papers; cascade routing reduced expensive verification volume [X]-fold, cutting cost per thousand units from $[A] to $[B].
  • Distilled an LLM judge into a fine-tuned encoder, retaining [X]% of macro F1 at [N]× lower inference cost and [M]× lower latency.
  • Reduced expected calibration error from [A] to [B] via isotonic regression, enabling a precision-targeted operating threshold at [X]% precision and [Y]% recall.
  • Achieved [X] macro F1 on a hand-labelled real-world holdout versus [Y] on synthetic benchmarks; analysed and reported the transfer gap.
  • Improved evidence retrieval nDCG@10 from [baseline] to [final] through hybrid fusion and cross-encoder reranking.
  • Independently measured outcome-switching prevalence at [X]% across [N] registered trials, consistent with published systematic review estimates.
  • Designed a ten-dimension evaluation harness wired into CI; eval-gated deployment blocked [N] regressions before merge.

Calibration, so progress stays legible: hybrid plus reranking should beat BM25 alone by roughly 15–30% nDCG@10; a synthetic-to-real F1 drop of 10–25 points is normal; distillation typically retains 85–95% of teacher performance; cost reduction of 40–70% through caching and routing is achievable. Substantially better than these on a first build usually means leakage.

Data and licensing

Handled at ingestion, never retrofitted

Metadata from Crossref, OpenAlex, Retraction Watch, and ClinicalTrials.gov is broadly redistributable. Full text is not uniformly so. Store full text only for open-access and licensed records, keep a pointer for everything else, and record the licence per record from day one.

Published outputs report aggregate prevalence and flag counts. Individual findings naming specific papers are held for human review before publication, because a false accusation of fabrication is a serious harm and the system's own documentation should say so.

No patient data is involved at any point.
